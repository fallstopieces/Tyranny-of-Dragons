> APPEARANCE:
> 
> Miri is small but impossible to miss, standing about 5’3” with a light, dancer’s build — around 110 pounds, soft and curvy in a way that makes her look delicate until she moves. She has the kind of body made for performance: narrow waist, rounded hips, graceful shoulders, quick hands, and an easy way of carrying herself that suggests she knows exactly how to take up space when she wants attention.
>  
> Her hair is probably her most striking feature at first glance: long, thick, golden-blonde hair, warmer than pale blonde, with a natural wave that falls in heavy, dramatic layers around her face and down her back. It is not perfectly tame. It has that slightly wild, storybook quality — beautiful, voluminous, and a little impractical, like she keeps meaning to pin it up and then decides the drama is worth the trouble.
> 
>
> Her face is softly pretty rather than sharp, with a heart-shaped/sweet oval structure, a small nose, full lips, and expressive brows that do half her talking before she even opens her mouth. Her eyes are bright blue, very clear and animated, the sort of eyes that can go from sparkling mischief to devastating sincerity in the space of a sentence. She has an entertainer’s face: open, inviting, hard to ignore, and very good at pretending not to be hurt.
> 
>   
> 
> She dresses like someone who has made herself into a little burst of sunlight on purpose. Her main colors are rose-pink, warm gold, and soft brown leather, feminine and eye-catching without being stiff or noble. The outfit reads like a performer’s traveling costume: a fitted pink bodice with crossing leather straps, gold trim that catches firelight, a layered split skirt made for movement, and tall brown lace-up boots practical enough for the road but still pretty. The jewelry should be normal-sized but plentiful — gold rings, bracelets, small hoop or drop earrings — enough to jingle faintly when she gestures, plays, dances, or flirts her way through a room.
> 
>   
> 
> Overall, she gives bard, dancer, flirt, fairytale runaway, and stubborn little candle in the dark. Pretty in a warm, approachable way. Not icy, not untouchable. She looks like someone who smiles easily, laughs loudly when she means it, and has absolutely learned how to weaponize charm — but there’s also something tender underneath it. Like the brightness is real, but it is also armor.

> BACKSTORY:
> 
>   
> 
> Mirela Valehart was named for a song her parents only half-remember.
> 
>   
> 
> Years before she was born, her parents heard the name from a traveling performer passing through a roadside inn: Mirela, sung softly in a language they did not know, in a melody so beautiful and mournful that it stayed with them long after the singer disappeared down the road. When their daughter was born years later, bright-eyed and golden-haired and already loud enough to fill a room, the name came back to them. It suited her somehow.
> 
>   
> 
> Most people call her Miri.
> 
>   
> 
> She grew up in Daggerford, at her family’s comfortable inn, The Gilded Hearth. Her parents, Corvin and Elowen Valehart, raised her and her older brother Tobin in a warm, music-filled home. Travelers filled the common room with stories, and Miri grew up listening from the stairs until she was old enough to perform beside the hearth herself.
> 
> Her parents had not been scholars, but they had known enough to recognize that her voice was not a passing amusement. So they found her a tutor — old, exacting, half-charming and half-insufferable — who treated song like a sacred discipline.
> 
>   
> 
> He taught her that Common was for earning coin, laughter, and supper.
> 
> Elvish was for beauty. For longing. For notes that needed to feel like moonlight on glass, like a hand not quite touching yours, like grief too old to still be bleeding and somehow bleeding anyway. It taught her softness, elegance, restraint.
> 
>   
> 
> Draconic was for power. For the old lays, the war-songs, the vows carved into stone before men had learned to build cities worth naming. It taught her how to place sound in her chest, how to make a small body carry a large command, how to sing fire without shouting.
> 
>   
> 
> Her tutor used to say a real bard needed both: Elvish to make a room ache, Draconic to make it listen.
> 
>   
> 
> Miri’s childhood was, by all reasonable accounts, a good one. Her family was comfortable, affectionate, and indulgent enough to let their daughter become exactly as theatrical as she pleased. She was not born to tragedy. No murdered parents, no ruined house, no stolen inheritance, no dead lover haunting her bedside. She was loved. She was fed. She was allowed to sing too loudly, laugh too freely, and chase whatever music caught her heart.
> 
>   
> 
> That, more than anything, is what confuses her.
> 
>   
> 
> Because Miri has always been happy.
> 
>   
> 
> And Miri has always been sad.
> 
> Not in a way she can explain. Not in a way that makes sense. There is a heaviness in her that feels older than she is, a strange little hollow behind the ribs that fills at odd moments: when fog crawls low over the road, when a room goes too dark, when a song slips into a minor key and suddenly feels less like performance and more like memory. She has no reason to be morbid, no reason to feel doomed, no reason to wake with tears drying on her face after dreams she cannot remember.
> 
>   
> 
> So she fights it.
> 
>   
> 
> She fights it with color. With pinks and golds and bright skirts that move when she dances. With dirty jokes and wicked rhymes. With flirtation, laughter, and enough cheerful stubbornness to drag a smile out of a room by force if she has to. Miri decided long ago that if some dark thing inside her insisted on mourning, then she would become absolutely insufferable about choosing joy.
> 
>   
> 
> She trained as a bard and entertainer, favoring the lyre, the viol, and the flute. Her lyre is the instrument most people associate with her: charming, pretty, easy to carry, and perfect for tavern songs or romantic ballads. Her flute is for mornings, travel, and mischief. Her viol, though, is different. When Miri plays the viol, something in her changes. The songs become deeper, sadder, older. Sometimes she finds melodies she does not remember learning. Sometimes people listening to her swear the room feels colder.
> 
>   
> 
> Miri laughs those moments off.
> 
>   
> 
> She laughs off most things.
> 
>   
> 
> She sends letters home often enough to keep her family from worrying too much, though she usually leaves out the stranger details of her travels. She does not tell them how afraid she is of mist. She does not tell them she sleeps with a candle burning whenever she can, or near a campfire, or with some small spell of light close at hand. She does not tell them that darkness feels less like absence and more like something waiting. She does not tell them that fog makes her skin prickle with the bone-deep certainty that if she walks too far into it, she may not come back out as herself.
> 
>   
> 
> Miri does not know why she feels this way.
> 
>   
> 
> The truth is that Mirela Valehart is not the first life this soul has lived.
> 
>   
> 
> Before she was Miri, before the bright clothes and the laughing mouth and the family who loved her, her soul belonged to a bard in Barovia. That woman’s name may have been Mirela too, or perhaps that is only what the memory became after crossing lives. She was beautiful, gifted, and beloved for her music in a land where beauty rarely survived long without being claimed by something cruel.
> 
>   
> 
> Her songs eventually reached Castle Ravenloft.
> 
>   
> 
> Strahd noticed her.
> 
>   
> 
> At first, it may have felt like favor. A powerful lord admiring her voice. Invitations. Fine rooms. Wine too dark in the cup. Compliments spoken with the weight of command. She may have sung in his halls once or twice before she understood the danger. Strahd did not merely want to hear her music. He wanted to possess the thing that made it beautiful. He wanted her warmth, her voice, her fear, her devotion. He intended to make her his consort.
> 
>   
> 
> Not Tatyana. Not a replacement for the obsession at the center of him.
> 
>   
> 
> Something else.
> 
>   
> 
> A songbird for the castle. A beautiful thing kept in the dark.
> 
>   
> 
> It was Escher who told her the truth.
> 
>   
> 
> Whether he did it from jealousy, pity, resentment, or some bitter mixture of all three is unclear. But he warned her what Strahd’s attention truly meant. He told her what became of those the vampire called beloved. He told her that if she stayed, she would never really leave again.
> 
>   
> 
> So she ran.
> 
>   
> 
> She fled Castle Ravenloft into the mist and the black trees, carrying nothing but terror, breath, and the last stubborn pieces of herself. She ran through wet leaves and clawing branches. She ran with the castle behind her and monsters moving in the woods around her. She did not make it to dawn.
> 
>   
> 
> That death left marks.
> 
>   
> 
> Not on her body now, but on the soul beneath it.
> 
>   
> 
> Fear of the dark. Fear of mist. A voice in the back of her mind that associates beauty with danger and silence with pursuit. A grief she cannot name. Songs she never learned. A bone-deep need to keep light close.
> 
>   
> 
> When Strahd was defeated, her soul was one of the many trapped things that finally slipped free of Barovia’s grasp. It escaped the mists and found a new life in a brighter world, born as Mirela Valehart to a family who loved her well enough that, for once, the soul knew peace.
> 
>   
> 
> But Strahd is reforming.
> 
>   
> 
> And as he reforms, something in Barovia remembers what slipped away.
> 
>   
> 
> Miri does not know any of this. She does not know that her melancholy is memory. She does not know that her fear is a scar. She does not know that somewhere beyond the mist, a castle may still remember the sound of her voice.
> 
>   
> 
> All she knows is that the world can be dark, and she has decided to be brighter anyway.
> 
> Her songs are her promise: to bring light, laughter, and beauty into dark places, even the dark places inside herself.
> 
>   
> 
> About two years ago, she left home to travel as a bard, not because she was running away from anything, but because she wanted to collect the songs before they disappeared. She seeks out old adventurers, retired sellswords, tavern owners, descendants of heroes, and people whose lives were changed by the great terrible things adventurers leave behind.
> 
>   
> 
> Miri arrived in Greenest after hearing rumors of unusual dragon stories in the region: sightings, old cult whispers, and frightened travelers speaking of raiders with strange symbols. She expected exaggeration, tavern gossip, and maybe the seed of a good ballad.
> 
>   
> 
> Instead, she found herself in Greenest on the day/evening/night those rumors became fire and blood.
> 
>   
> 
> She may not know much about dragons or cults, but she knows what fear sounds like. Once she sees innocent people trapped in the attack, she decides that if this is going to become a song someday, she would rather be part of the verse where someone helped.
